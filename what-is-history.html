A big question worth chasing

What is history?

Sixty-five thousand years of stories happened right here, under your feet. Here is how we know about them, and why it matters that you do.

Question One

What is history?

History is the study of the past. But it is also something people make: the careful, curious work of turning leftover clues into a story we can trust.

Think firstIf your whole life so far is "the past", which bits of it would end up in a history book? Who would decide?

A definition to hold on to

History (noun): the study of past events, especially in human affairs; and the written or spoken record we build from the evidence those events left behind.

The word comes from the ancient Greek historia, which means "inquiry" or "finding things out by asking". History has always been a question before it was ever an answer.

The past is everything

Every meal, every argument, every walk to the river, every thought that anyone ever had. It is far too big to hold, and almost all of it left no trace at all.

History is what we can work out

Historians gather the pieces that survived, test them, and fit them together. Some pieces are missing forever. That is not a failure. It is the puzzle, and it is why there is always more to find.

“
To be ignorant of what happened before you were born is to remain forever a child.
Cicero, Roman writer and politician, 46 BCE

Eight words that unlock the whole subject

Learn these and you can talk about any moment in history, anywhere in the world.

Source

Anything left behind that tells us something. A diary, a spear, a song, a photo, a shopping list.

Evidence

What a source proves once you have asked hard questions of it. Sources become evidence when you use them well.

Chronology

The order things happened in. Getting the order right is the first job, because it changes what caused what.

Cause and effect

Why something happened, and what happened next because of it. Big events almost always have several causes.

Continuity and change

What stayed the same and what became different. Both are interesting. Sometimes nothing changing is the story.

Perspective

The point of view someone had, shaped by who they were, where they stood and what they cared about.

Significance

Why an event matters, to whom, and for how long. Significance can change as the years go by.

Contestability

Historians disagree, on purpose. Arguing carefully about the evidence is how history gets better.

Question Three

What does a historian do?

Think detective, not librarian. A historian finds clues, questions witnesses, tests the story, and only then says what they think happened, and how sure they are.

Think firstA detective and a historian both work out what happened from clues. What is the one big advantage a detective has? What is the one big advantage a historian has?

Ask a real question

Not "tell me about the gold rush", but "why did so many people leave the goldfields with less money than they arrived with?" A sharp question does half the work.

Go hunting

Archives, museums, libraries, family cupboards, digital collections, the ground itself, and above all the people who hold the knowledge.

Read very closely

Slowly, twice. What does the source actually say, before you decide what it means? Historians read handwriting, old spelling, and languages that no longer sound the same.

Interrogate it

Who made this? When? Why? Who were they talking to? What did they want the reader to believe? And crucially, who is missing from it?

Cross-check everything

One source is a claim. Two sources that agree, made by people who did not talk to each other, is the beginning of evidence.

Build it, share it, revise it

Write the argument. Show the working. Then listen when someone finds a better source, and change your mind gladly. That is the job, not a failure at it.

The five questions that crack any source

Use these on a 200-year-old letter, or on something you saw online this morning. They work on both.

  1. Who made this? What did they know, and what could they not have known?
  2. When was it made? On the day, or fifty years later from memory?
  3. Why was it made? To record, to persuade, to sell, to boast, to complain, to remember?
  4. Who was it for? People tell a boss something different from what they tell a friend.
  5. Who is missing? Whose voice is not in this room, and why not?

Where this kind of thinking takes people

Historical thinking is really just high-quality evidence handling. Nearly every job that involves deciding what is true uses it.

History is not the past. It is the method we have evolved of organising our ignorance of the past.

Question Four

What are important events in Australia's history?

Ten moments that changed what happened next. Australia's story does not start in 1788. It starts tens of thousands of years earlier, and it is still being written.

Think firstWhat makes an event "important"? Is it how many people it affected, how long the effects lasted, or how much it changed what people believed? Historians argue about this constantly.

65,000+ years ago

The First Peoples, and the oldest continuing cultures on Earth

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have lived on this continent for at least 65,000 years, according to evidence from the Madjedbebe rock shelter in Arnhem Land. Over that time they developed hundreds of languages, sophisticated land management with fire, engineered fish traps, complex trade routes and knowledge systems carried in song, story and Country.

Why it mattersThis is the foundation of Australia's history, not an introduction to it. No other living culture on the planet has a longer unbroken record.
1606 and before

Visitors from the north and the first recorded European landfall

Makassan sailors from Sulawesi came south each year to harvest sea cucumber, trading and living alongside Yolŋu people in Arnhem Land, leaving words, songs and tamarind trees behind. In 1606 the Dutch ship Duyfken, captained by Willem Janszoon, made the first recorded European landing, on Cape York.

Why it mattersAustralia was never isolated. Long before Britain arrived, this continent was already connected to the world by sea, by trade and by friendship.
1770

James Cook charts the east coast

Cook sailed HMB Endeavour up the eastern coastline, mapping it in remarkable detail, meeting Gweagal people at Kamay (Botany Bay) and running aground on the Great Barrier Reef. In August he claimed the coast for Britain at Possession Island.

Why it mattersThose charts made British settlement possible. It is also a superb example of one event that is remembered very differently depending on who is telling it.
26 January 1788

The First Fleet arrives at Sydney Cove

Eleven ships carrying around 1,400 people, most of them convicts, arrived under Captain Arthur Phillip and founded a British penal colony on Gadigal land. For the peoples already living here it was the beginning of invasion, dispossession, disease and profound loss.

Why it mattersTwo entirely truthful stories begin on the same day. Understanding how both can be true is one of the most important things history teaches.
1851

Gold is found, and everything speeds up

Gold discoveries at Ophir in New South Wales and then Ballarat and Bendigo in Victoria set off a rush. People arrived from Britain, Ireland, China, America and Europe. Australia's non-Indigenous population more than doubled in a decade, and tents became cities.

Why it mattersThe gold rushes built Melbourne, brought Australia its first large Chinese communities, and created a confident, argumentative population that started asking for a say.
3 December 1854

The Eureka Stockade at Ballarat

Goldminers, furious about expensive licences and having no vote, built a stockade and swore an oath under a new blue and white flag. Soldiers stormed it before dawn and at least twenty-seven people died. Within a couple of years, almost every demand the miners had made was granted.

Why it mattersA short, violent morning that Australians have argued about ever since: was it a rebellion, a massacre, or the birth of Australian democracy? Historians still disagree.
1 January 1901

Federation: six colonies become one nation

After years of conventions, campaigns and referendums, New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, South Australia, Western Australia and Tasmania joined together as the Commonwealth of Australia, with its own constitution and parliament. Edmund Barton became the first Prime Minister.

Why it mattersA nation was created by argument and voting rather than by war, which is genuinely rare. That same parliament also passed laws that excluded people by race, and undoing that took most of the century.
25 April 1915

Gallipoli and the First World War

Australian and New Zealand troops landed at Gallipoli in what is now Turkey. The campaign failed and around 8,700 Australians died there. Out of a population of fewer than five million, more than 60,000 Australians died in the war overall.

Why it mattersAnzac Day grew from this into Australia's most widely observed national day. It also shows how nations choose which memories to build an identity on.
27 May 1967

The Referendum

Australians were asked whether Aboriginal people should be counted in the census and whether the federal parliament should be able to make laws for them. More than 90 per cent voted yes, the highest yes vote in any Australian referendum, after a ten-year campaign led largely by Aboriginal activists.

Why it mattersIt did not fix everything, and campaigners always said so. But it showed that ordinary Australians could be persuaded to change the country's founding document, overwhelmingly.
3 June 1992

The Mabo decision

Eddie Koiki Mabo and four other Meriam plaintiffs took a ten-year case to the High Court, arguing that their people had always owned Mer (Murray Island) in the Torres Strait. The Court agreed, and rejected the old legal fiction of terra nullius, the idea that Australia had belonged to nobody.

Why it mattersAustralian law finally recognised that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples had their own systems of law and land ownership all along. Native title exists because of it.

Question Five

Who are important people in Australia's past?

Ten lives that bent the story. Some led armies of one. Some changed the law. Some simply refused to accept that things had to stay the way they were.

Think firstMost people who changed history never became famous. Who in your own family or suburb has done something that mattered, that no book will ever mention?

Pemulwuy
Bidjigal · c.1750 to 1802

A Bidjigal warrior from the Botany Bay area who led resistance against the British colony for twelve years, with raids, tactics and a reputation that made him legendary on both sides.

He fought for his Country when almost nobody else could.
Woollarawarre Bennelong
Wangal · c.1764 to 1813

Taken captive by Governor Phillip, he became a skilled go-between across two worlds, learned English, sailed to Britain, met King George III and came home again. He wrote the earliest known letter in English by an Aboriginal person.

A diplomat in an impossible position, and far more than a footnote.
David Unaipon
Ngarrindjeri · 1872 to 1967

Inventor, writer, preacher and scientist. He filed nineteen patent applications, including an improved shearing handpiece, wrote the first book published by an Aboriginal author, and spent decades arguing publicly for Aboriginal rights.

Look at an Australian fifty dollar note. That is him.
Vincent Lingiari
Gurindji · c.1919 to 1988

In 1966 he led two hundred Gurindji stockmen and their families off Wave Hill Station over pay and conditions, then kept walking for nine years over something bigger: their land. In 1975 the Prime Minister poured a handful of soil into his palm and handed it back.

A strike that turned into the land rights movement.
Eddie Koiki Mabo
Meriam · 1936 to 1992

A gardener at James Cook University who was told, in a conversation over lunch, that his family did not legally own their land on Mer. He said that could not be right, and spent ten years proving it in court. He died five months before he won.

One stubborn person changed Australian law forever.
Faith Bandler
Australian South Sea Islander · 1918 to 2015

Her father was kidnapped from Vanuatu and forced to work in Queensland cane fields. She grew up to lead the ten-year campaign for the 1967 referendum, travelling the country persuading ordinary Australians to vote yes. More than nine in ten did.

She proved that patient campaigning can shift a whole nation.
Sir Edmund Barton
First Prime Minister · 1849 to 1920

He spent more than a decade travelling, arguing and drafting to persuade six separate colonies to become one country, then became Australia's first Prime Minister in 1901 and later a founding judge of the High Court.

He also helped pass laws that excluded people by race, which Australians spent the next century undoing.
Edith Cowan
First woman in an Australian parliament · 1861 to 1932

A social reformer who campaigned for women's and children's welfare, helped found a children's court, and in 1921 became the first woman elected to any Australian parliament. In her first speech she pointed out how strange it was that she was the only one.

She is on the fifty dollar note too. Look at the other side.
Howard Florey
Scientist · 1898 to 1968

An Adelaide-born scientist who took Alexander Fleming's forgotten mould and, with his Oxford team, turned it into penicillin, a medicine that could actually be made and given to people. It arrived just in time for the Second World War.

Often described as the Australian whose work saved the most lives, by a very long way.
Fred Hollows
Eye surgeon · 1929 to 1993

He was appalled by the rates of blindness he found in remote Aboriginal communities and did something about it, then took the same work to Nepal, Eritrea and Vietnam, and set up factories so poor countries could make their own cheap lenses.

Millions of people can see because of decisions he made.

Question Six

Do people have different perspectives about history?

Always. And here is the surprising part: that is not a problem with history. It is the most interesting thing about it.

Think firstThink of the last argument you had with a sibling or friend. If you both wrote down what happened, would the two accounts match? Were either of you lying?

Start with something that happened at lunchtime

A handball game. Someone slams the ball, it hits another player in the face, and there is a lot of shouting. Four people saw it. Here is what each of them honestly remembers.

The one who hit the ball

"It was a normal shot. She stepped forward into it. I called out sorry straight away and nobody listened to me."

The one who was hit

"He smashed it at me on purpose. He has been doing it all week. It really hurt and everyone just laughed."

The teacher on duty

"I was on the far side near the canteen. I heard a bang and then screaming. By the time I got there, three children were crying."

His best friend

"She had already been arguing with him. Honestly, she kind of started it. He would never do that on purpose."

Why the accounts differ

Nobody here is lying. They differ because of where they were standing, what they could see and hear, what they already believed about each other, and what they wanted to be true. Change any one of those and you get a different honest account.

Now scale it up

Now imagine the event is a war, or a ship arriving, or a law being passed, and the witnesses are separated by two hundred years, several languages and a great deal at stake. That is exactly what historians work with, every single day.

The Australian example everybody knows

One date. 26 January 1788. The very same event, remembered in completely different ways by people who are all Australians.

Australia Day

For many Australians the date marks the beginning of the country they live in: the arrival that led to the towns, the farms, the parliament, the migration story, the flag and the barbecue on the beach. It is a day for celebrating what has been built and who has come to share it.

Invasion Day, or Survival Day

For many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people the same date marks the start of invasion: land taken, families broken, languages lost, thousands of deaths. Some call it Survival Day instead, because their cultures are still here, still strong, more than two centuries later.

The rule that stops this getting silly: having different perspectives does not mean every claim is equally true. "Some people found the day joyful and some found it devastating" is perspective, and both are real. "Nobody was living here in 1788" is not a perspective, it is simply wrong, and the evidence buries it. Historians weigh evidence. They do not shrug.

Perspective

An honest view shaped by who you are and where you stood. Several can be true at once.

Bias

A lean in one direction, often without meaning to. Everyone has it. Naming it is how you handle it.

Propaganda

Deliberately shaping what people believe. Still a brilliant source, just not about what it claims to be about.

“
Until the lion learns to write, every story will glorify the hunter.
African proverb

This is why the best question a young historian can ask is not "what happened?" but "whose voice is missing, and how could I go and find it?" A great deal of the most exciting history being written right now is exactly that: people finding the lions.

Question Seven

Should we learn about history? Why?

Yes. Not because it is on the timetable, but because it hands you eight things that are genuinely hard to get any other way.

The greatest story ever told

Empires, shipwrecks, escapes, inventions, betrayals, sixty-five thousand years of people being brave and clever and ridiculous. And it is not a made-up story. It actually happened, on the ground you are standing on.

A shield against being fooled

History trains you to ask who made this, why, and what they left out. That is precisely the skill you need against dodgy headlines, edited videos and confident people online who are simply making things up.

It explains why things are the way they are

Why the borders are there. Why we drive on the left. Why some suburbs have money and others do not. Why certain days are holidays. None of it is random. All of it has a reason, and the reason is history.

You get to live a thousand lives

Standing in someone else's shoes across two centuries and half a world is the best empathy training ever invented. Understanding why people did things you find shocking is not the same as excusing them. It is much harder, and much more useful.

It tells you where you come from

Your family, your suburb, your school, your Country. Every one of them has a story with you at the end of it, and most of those stories have never been written down by anyone. Somebody has to. It might as well be you.

It proves that things can change

Every rule you think is permanent was once new, argued over, and opposed. Women could not vote. Children worked in mines. If it was different before, it can be different again. History is the strongest evidence there is that the future is not fixed.

It is full of unsolved mysteries

Where did the Mahogany Ship go? What happened to Ludwig Leichhardt? Whose footprints are at Willandra Lakes? Nobody knows. These are real open questions, and some of them will be answered by people who are twelve right now.

It makes you formidable

Reading closely, weighing evidence, spotting a weak argument, writing something people believe. Employers pay a great deal for those, and no software has taken them over yet. History students end up as lawyers, journalists, curators, film-makers and leaders.

"If you don't know history, then you don't know anything. You are a leaf that doesn't know it is part of a tree."

Michael Crichton, novelist

"History, despite its wrenching pain, cannot be unlived, but if faced with courage, need not be lived again."

Maya Angelou, poet

"History is the memory of things said and done."

Carl Becker, historian

You are already a historian

You have told someone what happened at lunchtime. You have worked out who ate the last biscuit from the crumbs. You have listened to a grandparent describe a world that no longer exists.

That is the whole job. Evidence, questions, care, and a story you can defend. The only difference is scale.

So: what is the one question about the past that you actually want the answer to? Start there. That is exactly how every historian who has ever lived got started.
