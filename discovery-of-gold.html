Week 7 · Lessons 1 and 2 · The Gold Rush
Why did five specks of gold in a creek change a whole continent?

Learning intention

To use primary and secondary sources to investigate how gold was discovered in 1851, where it was found, and what its discovery set in motion.

Success criteria

  • I can retell the discovery story in order and say who was really involved.
  • I can find Sydney, Bathurst, Ophir, Melbourne, Ballarat and Bendigo on a map and explain a digger’s journey.
  • I can describe what this Country was to Aboriginal people before 1851 and what the rush did to it.
  • I can name early consequences for the creek, the town, the colony and the world.
  • I can ask the four Source Detective questions of a picture, a number and a video.

Card 4 of 34
Lesson 1 · Country · the 1820s and 1830s

Sheep first

Squatters drove sheep onto the grassy plains. The sheep ate out the murnong and trampled the waterholes.

At Bathurst the Wiradjuri fought back. In 1824 the governor declared martial law there: soldiers were sent against them.

Disease came with the settlers too.

!Know this

The plains were already damaged before any gold was found. The rush came on top of that.

✎In your book

One sentence: Sheep damaged the land because…

Card 5 of 34
Lesson 1 · Country · after 1851

Then the diggers

The diggers dug up the creeks and turned the water to mud. They cut down the trees. They shot the animals. They dug through places that were sacred.

“Miners have two especial propensities, those of firing guns and felling trees.”
William Howitt, an English writer who visited the diggings in the 1850s
!Know this

The Dja Dja Wurrung have a name for what the diggers made: “upside down country”.

✎In your book

Write upside down country. Then write one sentence saying what the diggers did to the land.

Card 6 of 34
Lesson 1 · Country · Source Detective

Two numbers

Wadawurrung people before the settlers came: about 3,240, in 25 clans.

Counted in 1861, ten years into the rush: 255.

Source Detective · a number is a source too
  • Who counted? Colonial officials. Not Wadawurrung people.
  • When? 1861. The 3,240 is a guess looking backwards.
  • Why? A government count, made to manage people.
  • What can it NOT tell us? Names. Families. People who hid from the count. What the survivors did next.
✎In your book

Write both numbers. Then finish this sentence: One thing these numbers cannot tell us is…

TeacherFirst time the girls treat a statistic as a source. The Week 10 population figures get the same four questions. Run the questions out loud before they write.

Lesson 1 · Country · not only a story of loss

On the goldfields too

Aboriginal people were on the goldfields as well:

  • Guides who knew the way and where the water was
  • Trackers and Native Police
  • Traders selling possum-skin rugs and fish to hungry diggers

A Wadawurrung man is credited with finding Ballarat’s richest gold, the Eureka Lead, while looking for a lost horse.

!Know this

Aboriginal people used the rush, on their own Country, even while it was destroying that Country.

1

California, 1849

Edward Hargraves is a big man from Gosford who has failed at most things. He sails to the Californian gold rush in 1849 and fails there too.

But he notices something. The hills and creeks of California look just like the country he once rode through behind Bathurst. He comes home to test the idea.

!Know this

Hargraves did not find gold by luck. He recognised the kind of country gold hides in.

✎In your book

Card 12 of 34
Lesson 1 · The Story
2

Five specks · 12 February 1851

Hargraves rides over the Blue Mountains to Guyong. An innkeeper’s son, John Lister, about 22, shows him the creeks.

At Lewis Ponds Creek Hargraves fills a tin dish with gravel, swirls it in the water, and finds five specks of gold.

“This is a memorable day in the history of New South Wales. I shall be a baronet, you will be knighted, and my old horse will be stuffed and sent to the British Museum.”
Hargraves, as he wrote it down himself years later  Reported
!Know this

12 February 1851: five specks at Lewis Ponds Creek, on Wiradjuri Country.

The people who did the work

Hargraves teaches Lister and the Tom brothers, William and James, to pan and to build a cradle. Then he leaves.

In April 1851 Lister and William Tom find four ounces of gold where the two creeks meet. That is payable gold: enough to live on. Hargraves takes their sample to Sydney.

Card 15 of 34
Lesson 1 · The Story
5

Why 1851 was different

By 1851 the fear has flipped. Ships full of young men are leaving Sydney for California. The colony is losing its workers anyway.

Better gold here than gold there. Hargraves asks the government for a reward. He names the field Ophir, after King Solomon’s land of gold in the Bible. The Sydney Morning Herald prints the news in May.

!Know this

1851: the government now WANTED people to find gold, to stop them leaving for California.

Card 16 of 34
Lesson 1 · The Story
6

Gold fever

By 15 May there are 300 diggers at Ophir. Within weeks, more than a thousand. In Sydney, shops shut and servants walk out.

“A complete mental madness appears to have seized almost every member of the community.”
Bathurst Free Press, May 1851

On 1 June the government brings in the gold licence: 30 shillings a month, gold or no gold.

!Know this

Gold fever: within weeks, a thousand people at one creek and a licence to pay every month.

Card 17 of 34
Lesson 1 · The Story · the end of beat 6

Who got the credit?

Hargraves was given £10,000. A farm worker earned about £50 a year.

Lister and the Toms got almost nothing.

In 1890, thirty-nine years later, a NSW parliamentary committee finally ruled that “Messrs Tom and Lister were undoubtedly the first discoverers of gold obtained in Australia in payable quantity.”

Pairs · 30 seconds, then hands upWho should have got the reward? Hargraves, Lister and the Toms, or shared?
!Know this

Two official sources disagree about the same event. The one that became “the history” was the one with the money behind it.

Victoria panics

On 1 July 1851 Port Phillip becomes its own colony: Victoria. Straight away it starts losing people north to Ophir.

Melbourne’s businessmen offer £200 to anyone who finds gold within 200 miles of Melbourne. It takes weeks: Clunes in July, Ballarat in August, and by the end of the year Bendigo and Mount Alexander, richer than anything at Ophir.

!Know this

Victoria’s goldfields were found within months of Ophir, and they were bigger.

8

Melbourne empties

“Cottages are deserted, houses to let, business is at a standstill, and even schools are closed. In some of the suburbs not a man is left.”
Charles La Trobe, Governor of Victoria, writing to London, October 1851

In plain words: houses empty, shops shut, schools closed, and hardly a man left in town.

Ships arriving at Melbourne lose their whole crews to the diggings and sit at anchor, unable to leave.

Lesson 2 · The Story continues
9

The world hears

Letters and newspapers carry the news to Britain, Ireland, Germany, America and southern China.

In 1852 alone, around 90,000 people land in Victoria. Melbourne, a town of 23,000, grows a suburb of tents along the Yarra. Australia goes from about 430,000 people in 1851 to 1.7 million by 1871.

!Know this

430,000 people in 1851. 1.7 million by 1871. The rush changed who Australians were.

Lesson 2 · Route Planner · Task C

Why is the gold in the creek?

Gold is very heavy. A piece of gold weighs about 19 times more than a piece of water the same size, and much more than sand.

For millions of years, rain has washed tiny bits of gold out of the mountains into the creeks. Where the water slows down (at a bend, behind a rock, where two creeks meet) the gold sinks to the bottom and stays there. The light sand washes away.

Gold found in a creek like this is called alluvial gold. That is why the first diggers only needed a tin dish, a cradle and a creek.

Task C · on your laptopSwitch the map to Terrain. Follow Lewis Ponds Creek uphill from Ophir. Where does the water come from?
!Know this

Gold sinks. Creeks sort it out of the gravel for free. That is why every rush began in a creek.

✎In your book

Write the word alluvial and explain in two sentences why gold ends up in creeks.
